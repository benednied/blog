# Software Engineers of the Possible

DHH’s Rails World keynote reminded me of the fire-sale speech in *Margin Call*. Honour the old craft, announce that its economics no longer work, and hand out the compensation packets. The party is over; get to work.

Software used to be compensated roughly like accounting. Then it became the West Coast’s answer to finance, creating a professional class with high salaries, equity, autonomy, and the belief that programmers were not ordinary employees.

Shortly before the dot-com crash, computer science stopped being merely a refuge for nerds. It became the responsible, moral path to wealth. People may have liked the work, but they also liked the life around it.

That is the car scene in *Margin Call*. Seth is terrified that he will be fired. Will tells him that it will hurt, and then he will be fine. Seth fears more than unemployment. He fears expulsion from the place where money, status, and identity have fused together. 

AI might be dissolving a similar bargain. Translating tickets into respectable code may no longer justify membership in an exceptional economic class.
SWEs are right to fear the reaper.
DHH proposes a new role for some: “professional makers of things.” But what things?

This year, I think I might have become capable of software engineering proper, although I still cannot program. I consider myself acquainted with hacker culture, but until recently I could barely put a nail into a wall with regards to the actual coding, so to speak. 
I could collect ideas from books; I could not turn them into repositories—nothing that worked, anyway.

AI has become my prosthesis. I now make software that does what it says on the cover: readable, documented, and subjected to every quality gate I can find and think of—`uv`, `ruff`, `ty`, tests, formatters, and static analysis. I care more about the machine that builds the machine than its output. Being the ideas guy has always come naturally; now I get to amass substance, and I cannot truly believe it just yet.

The belief that there is anything of substance, though, is probably a fallacy. A beautiful factory can produce something nobody wants. Internal coherence is seductive because it can be measured; taste cannot. A passing test suite proves that the program behaves as specified. It does not prove that the specification matters.

Agents have nevertheless narrowed the distance between recognizing an idea and constructing it. I could read *The Dream Machine* or *Dealers of Lightning* and admire the people who imagined personal computers, networks, mice, and windows while sensible engineers worked at IBM. Admiration did not confer productive capacity. Now it sometimes does.

These early hackers were intensely driven, weird at best, and socially outcast at worst. I have always felt drawn to them, but we should ask whether we are kidding ourselves when we claim them as ancestors. They were not optimizing for employability. They were unable to leave their visions unbuilt.

This is why taste and hacker culture are the same thing. Taste is seeing what ought to exist before conventional wisdom can describe it. Hacker culture is building it before conventional wisdom grants permission. A hacker may automate his home to the detriment of hygiene and sleep. Or he may release, under the MIT license, something offensively destructive to the software job market: credit card in, Jira in, pull request out—a genuine junior-developer replacement. Originality is not a personal brand. It does leave evidence.

Hackers are the engineers of the possible: discovering tools, opening paths, challenging inherited limits, and turning imagination into material reality. Agents supply productive force, but cannot decide what deserves to exist.

Before the internet became an industry, it was a strange new fabric for computing, one that curious people kept finding new uses for. Agents feel like such an opening now. It would be a strange kind of hacker who turned it down.

To cut back to the opener: near the end of *Margin Call*, the bank’s CEO, John Tuld, explains his actual skill is hearing where the music might go next—in a week, a month, or a year. Very few people seem to have an ear for where software engineering is going. Not the engineers, and not the executives who employ them.

Some hackers had that gift, though. Bob Metcalfe helped invent Ethernet and then built 3Com on the bet that the world would need it.

If you are a Tuld, you will be fine in the age of agents. Anyone who can repeatedly anticipate the next movement has always been able to make money in nearly any field. AI changes the instrument or the genre, not that advantage.

I do not know whether AI has made me a hacker or merely allowed me to produce immaculate evidence that I am not one. Only time—and people finding my software useful—will tell whether I can hear the music. But I can now place bets on my taste. If I manage to remain in technology, hedge enough to survive being wrong, and act entrepreneurially when something works, one of those bets might pay spectacularly.

George Hotz put the alternative bluntly: “Get out of technology.” If technology means comfortable employment that pays like finance, he may be right. If it means hackers building genuinely new things, this may be the best moment in decades.
